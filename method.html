How the estimator works, and how accurate it is

Fine-tuning and inference cost estimates built on our own measurements: 155 fine-tuning runs and 62 serving runs on 6 GPU types (A100, H100, H200, B200, L40S, RTX 4090), 1 to 8 GPUs, 1.7B to 70B models. Every accuracy number below is on held-out data the model did not see.

The profiler-informed model below was evaluated offline on the same runs; it is the next version of the estimator, not yet what the calculator runs.

The approach

  1. Measure the hardware once, cheaply. A 10-minute profiler measures each GPU's real matrix-multiply speed, memory bandwidth, attention speed (Flash and standard kernels) and GPU-to-GPU bandwidth. Datasheets overstate what GPUs deliver, unevenly: the L40S reaches 54% of its datasheet matmul rate, the RTX 4090 95%.
  2. Physics first. Speed starts from a roofline: the compute a training step needs (6 x active parameters per token) against the GPU's measured throughput.
  3. Learn the efficiency, not the answer. A regression learns how far real runs fall short of the roofline from the setup: tokens per step, model size, method (LoRA, QLoRA, DPO, full), checkpointing, attention kernel, optimizer, MoE, DDP or FSDP across GPUs.
  4. Correct what is left with a Gaussian process. It captures interactions the regression misses and gives each estimate an uncertainty.
  5. Bound the price. A conformal bound on held-out errors sets the price cap; its coverage is tested by calibrating without the GPU being checked, and reported per GPU because it is uneven.

This follows the operator-profiling idea of Vidur (MLSys 2024) and the cross-GPU transfer idea of Habitat (USENIX ATC 2021), extended to fine-tuning, which neither covers, and tied to a priced guarantee.

The chosen model is the best of 12 variants (6 models x 2 feature sets) scored on these same folds, not selected in a separate inner loop, so its numbers are slightly optimistic. The unseen-GPU test hides that GPU's training runs but still uses its profiler numbers from the same host; host-to-host variation (about 10%) is therefore not in the test. Checked against the older SDS runs on other hosts with real data, it scores 14.3% mean and 16.0% median. The previous calculator is a fixed formula, not refitted, so it scores the same in every column. The chosen model is best on unseen GPUs and unseen setups; on unseen models the plain physics regression is better (25.8% against 30.7%), so for model families we have not measured the physics regression is the better choice.

On an unseen GPU, per-token time is predicted about as well by physics alone as by any learned correction. On GPUs we have measured, the calculator does apply a per-GPU correction fitted on our own vLLM runs of that GPU. Throughput improves with a learned correction. First-token time under load is not predictable from these features: it is dominated by queueing once users exceed capacity. That needs a request-level scheduler simulation (the Vidur component), which is the next piece of work.

Sanity checks we ran

  • Profiler pieces add up: one layer's matrix multiplies plus attention match the measured whole-layer time within 0 to 16% on every GPU (the rest is normalisation and element-wise work).
  • Measured GPU-to-GPU bandwidth on 8x H100 (362 GB/s) matches the value the traffic model uses (360 GB/s).

Known limits

  • Unseen models are the weak case (30.7% MAPE): one MoE model, and the smallest models were only run with full fine-tuning. More model families are the fix.
  • No AMD or Apple training runs yet; MI300X had no capacity during this campaign.
  • Speeds are measured on RunPod hosts; the same GPU varies about 10% between hosts, which the price cap has to absorb.
  • Startup (container, model download) is measured separately: 38 s to 9 min to reach a ready pod, and about 50 s to download a 16 GB model.
